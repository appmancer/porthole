# PORTHOLE Level Campaign Plan

This document brings together the current thinking on the campaign.

The exact level count is not sacred. The current plan is to build roughly 20
levels, then keep the strongest final set for the product, likely 16-17 levels.

## Campaign Principles

- Small levels should default to 4 rooms.
- Medium levels should default to 6 rooms.
- Large levels should default to 8-9 rooms.
- Every level needs a clear identity, not just a mechanic list.
- Every level should be describable in one sentence.
- Every level should have a memorable moment to build around.
- Weak levels are usually weak because they are cramped, over-fiddly, or do not
  have a strong enough identity.

## Level Types

- `Teach`: introduce a mechanic or puzzle language cleanly.
- `Explore`: combine a mechanic with older ideas and let it breathe.
- `Examine`: demand planning, mastery, or a deeper reinterpretation of known tools.

## Campaign Arc

- Levels 1-6: foundational teaching arc
- Levels 7-11: broader puzzle language and stronger level identities
- Levels 12-14: established mid/late-game anchors
- Levels 15-21: advanced and capstone material

## Current Problems By Pattern

### Cramped Small Levels

The old 2-room levels tend to suffer from the same issues:

- corridors become cramped
- rooms do too much at once
- ideas do not get enough setup and payoff space
- fiddliness replaces clarity

Decision:

- 4 rooms is the new baseline for a small level

### Weak Levels Usually Fail For One Of Three Reasons

- no clear identity
- too much precision/fiddliness for the campaign position
- too many ideas in too little space

## Likely Cut Candidates

If 20 levels are built and only 16 are kept, the most vulnerable current levels
are:

- Level 5
- Level 6
- Level 9
- Level 10

Not because they have no value, but because their identities are least complete
in their current forms.

## Design Checklist For Each Level

Before a level is considered finished, it should answer all of these:

- What is the one-sentence identity?
- Is it `Teach`, `Explore`, or `Examine`?
- What is the player supposed to realise?
- What is the memorable moment?
- Does the current layout actually deliver that moment?
- What can be cut if the level starts becoming cramped or fiddly?

## Recommended Next Production Steps

1. Rebuild Levels 1-6 as proper 4-room small teaching levels.
2. Sharpen the identity of Levels 7-11, especially Level 11 as `Crossfire`.
3. Preserve Levels 12-14 as strong anchors, with polish focused on fairness and space.
4. Design new levels 15-21 around the missing themes instead of adding more miscellaneous content.
5. Playtest the full set, then choose the strongest final campaign based on identity, readability, and memorable payoff.

## Early-Game Arc Revision

The early campaign has now been restructured around six clean teaching levels:

1. `Level 1 - Orientation`
   - focus on horizontal movement
   - introduce buttons and barriers
   - lots of opportunity to portal, but portal use does not need to be mandatory

2. `Level 2 - Portal Surfaces`
   - teach portalable vs unportalable walls
   - teach basic aiming before the reticle is introduced
   - then layer in reticle use as a cleaner aiming aid
   - focus on portal literacy, not hazard management

3. `Level 3 - Descent`
   - focus on moving down, not up
   - let gravity be Chell's friend
   - zappers can appear as readable movement hazards
   - avoid cubes and complex object logic

4. `Level 4 - First Real Puzzle`
   - introduce cubes
   - transition from movement teaching to real problem solving
   - ask for reasoning beyond simply pressing a button

5. `Level 5 - State Change`
   - teach changing the room and then using that change
   - make sequence and transformed routes the point

6. `Level 6 - Fling`
   - teach deliberate momentum conversion
   - keep it distinct from descent

This creates a cleaner early campaign structure:

- Level 1: move across
- Level 2: learn the portal gun
- Level 3: move down
- Level 4: solve systems
- Level 5: change the room, then use it
- Level 6: exploit momentum

It also keeps the early levels from becoming cramped by trying to teach too many
things at once.
